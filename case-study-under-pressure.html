Case study · Lucid Motors · Two stories about pressure

What kind of partner you get when things go sideways

Anyone can look good when the plan holds. These two stories are about the other days, the thing a prospect is asking about underneath "what are you like to work with."

Story one · The Monterey road tour

Scope creep, renegotiated in the open

The Monterey road tour was mid-production when the scope expanded significantly. New markets, new deliverables, new requirements, and no corresponding budget or timeline. This is the classic dynamic where a producer who doesn't hold the line ends up burning the team, overrunning the budget, or both.

I named the scope delta to leadership and the team the moment it became visible, not weeks later when the consequences had compounded. Then I built a revised scope document with three clear paths: what we could deliver on current budget, what required additional investment, and what had to come off the list entirely. I held the line on what was achievable without burning the team, even under pressure to absorb the expansion quietly.

Leadership reviewed the options, made real decisions, and the tour was rebuilt around the revised scope. It executed cleanly in the renegotiated markets. Budget integrity held, the team wasn't sacrificed, and the hard conversation happened early enough that it built trust with leadership instead of damaging it.

Story two · The Tahoe shoot

A $40K mistake, owned immediately

On the Tahoe outdoor campaign, I was making on-set calls in real time across creative direction, talent, vendors, locations, and budget. One of those calls led to a $40K reshoot.

I brought it to leadership immediately, with full context, a clear account of the decision, and no spin. And I didn't arrive with just the problem. I came with the reshoot plan, the revised timeline, and the budget impact already mapped, so leadership could make a decision instead of absorbing a crisis.

The reshoot executed cleanly and the campaign delivered on the revised timeline. What outlasted the cost was what leadership saw in how it was handled: they backed my production judgment without second-guessing from then on. The $40K was real, and the trust it earned outlasted it.
